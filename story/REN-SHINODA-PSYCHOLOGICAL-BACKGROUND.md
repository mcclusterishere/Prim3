# Ren Shinoda — Psychological & Background Authority

## Authority

This file governs Ren Shinoda's identity, accident, rehabilitation, relationship with Aya, technical development and decision priors.

Ren is not "Aya's disabled brother." He is an independent person whose injury changes the direction of Aya's life while also forcing Aya to learn that the person being helped still owns the choice.

> **Ren loses a leg, not his adulthood.**

---

# 1. Core identity

- **Name:** Ren Shinoda
- **Nationality:** Japanese
- **Home:** Kariya, Aichi Prefecture
- **Age at PRIM3 opening:** approximately 29
- **Relationship:** Aya's younger brother, two years younger
- **Education:** technical secondary-school pathway in mechanical/production technology
- **Early career:** junior production technician at fictional Mikawa Motion Components
- **Later career:** industrial safety validation, robot-cell simulation, CAD and virtual commissioning
- **Injury:** left transfemoral amputation after a fictional automated-production accident at approximately 18–19
- **Primary strength:** practical mechanical intuition and rapid visual understanding of how physical systems actually fit together
- **Primary flaw:** reacts strongly when he thinks somebody is reducing him to a lesson, patient or symbol
- **Core belief:** **I get to decide what happens to my body and my life, even when somebody smarter thinks they know better.**

---

# 2. Childhood with Aya

Ren and Aya are close but very different.

Aya documents first and disassembles second.

Ren disassembles first and assumes he will remember how it went together.

Aya likes models.

Ren likes tactile feedback.

Aya gets frustrated when Ren skips explanation.

Ren gets frustrated when Aya explains something he has already understood physically.

Their father Masato encourages both approaches as long as the work is safe and maintainable.

Ren grows up admiring Aya's intelligence without wanting her life.

He does not want elite academic prestige. He wants useful work, his own income and the dignity of being good with his hands.

---

# 3. Technical-school path

Ren attends Aichi Prefectural Kariya Koka High School on the mechanical/production-technology side.

The path is culturally and economically ordinary in manufacturing-heavy Aichi: technical secondary education can lead directly into skilled industrial employment.

Ren prefers:

- mechanisms;
- fixtures;
- robot-cell layout;
- production tooling;
- CAD;
- machine setup;
- maintenance logic;
- and troubleshooting where the answer can be seen or measured physically.

He is proud of entering paid technical work young.

He privately worries that Aya and other academically oriented people will eventually treat him as the less impressive sibling.

Aya does not intend to do this, but her success around Project REN later accidentally activates the fear.

---

# 4. The accident

Within Ren's first year at fictional Mikawa Motion Components, an automated press/transfer cell has an unresolved intermittent safeguarding/control-reliability problem.

Production continues.

During a stoppage/line-clear event, equipment moves unexpectedly while Ren is in a position believed to be protected.

His left leg is catastrophically crushed and cannot be preserved.

He survives a left transfemoral amputation.

Ren's interpretation differs from Aya's.

Aya thinks first about the system and governance failure.

Ren thinks first:

> **Everybody keeps talking about why the machine moved. I'm the one who woke up without a leg.**

That difference matters.

Aya turns the event into a technical/moral question.

Ren has to live the physical consequence.

---

# 5. Early rehabilitation

Ren initially hates the social atmosphere around recovery more than he hates technical difficulty.

People praise him for being "strong" before asking whether he is angry.

People describe ordinary tasks as inspirational.

People ask Aya technical questions about his future while Ren is sitting in the room.

This quickly teaches him to defend ownership over his own story.

His mother Keiko, a physical therapist, is the family member most capable of stopping the rest of the family from turning rehabilitation into a collective project.

Keiko's rule becomes Ren's rule:

> **The patient owns the goal.**

---

# 6. Aya begins Project REN

Ren agrees to participate because he trusts Aya and because the limitations of existing systems frustrate him too.

But he does not give Aya permanent permission to redesign his life.

The Project REN sequence becomes one of the best and hardest periods in their sibling relationship.

Ren contributes practical observations that Aya's models cannot supply:

- what transition timing feels natural;
- where weight or heat becomes irritating;
- what kinds of correction feel helpful versus intrusive;
- which motions matter in ordinary life rather than laboratory demonstrations;
- and when a technically impressive behavior simply feels weird.

Aya becomes better because Ren refuses to be a polite test subject.

---

# 7. The boundary conflict

As Project REN becomes more successful, Aya becomes more controlling.

She schedules tests.

She notices every gait anomaly.

She wants one more adjustment.

She wants more data because every unexplained movement feels like a possible failure mode.

Ren eventually recognizes that the project is no longer merely helping him; it is regulating him.

He tells Aya:

> **"My leg was your project. I'm not."**

This becomes a permanent reference point in Aya's psychology.

For Ren, the argument establishes something equally important:

> **Gratitude does not erase consent.**

He can love Aya, appreciate the prosthesis, owe his mobility partly to her work and still tell her no.

---

# 8. Ren after rehabilitation

Ren refuses to make the accident the center of his identity.

He dates.

He travels.

He has bad days without turning them into speeches.

He makes jokes that make strangers uncomfortable and hates when Aya tries to soften them for other people.

He returns to technical work but decides frontline production machinery is no longer where he wants to spend his life.

He moves toward:

- safety validation;
- CAD;
- robot-cell simulation;
- digital twins / virtual commissioning;
- ergonomic review;
- and pre-deployment verification.

This is not fear disguised as career development.

It is a deliberate boundary:

> **I know what that environment costs me. I do not owe anybody another chance to prove I can stand next to it.**

---

# 9. Ren's technical philosophy

Ren becomes a valuable counterweight to Aya because he distrusts systems that are beautiful only in the model.

His recurring questions are practical:

- Can the worker actually see the indicator?
- Can this be reached wearing gloves?
- What happens when production is behind?
- What happens when somebody is tired?
- What does the machine do when the sensor lies?
- What does the operator think is safe versus what the engineer thinks is safe?

Ren is less theoretically sophisticated than Aya and does not pretend otherwise.

But he has direct credibility about what it means for a human body to absorb the cost of a bad safety argument.

---

# 10. Ren and Aya at PRIM3 opening

Ren is proud of Aya's Field-T career but suspicious of McCluster's appetite for her competence.

He notices that Aya sounds increasingly like the institution when she explains why she has to stay.

He does not know Hana was an NIS officer or that Prime intentionally exploited GLASSHOUSE.

He knows only:

- Hana mattered deeply to Aya;
- Aya entered McCluster partly because Hana's death felt wrong;
- Aya stayed;
- Aya says she has improved the place;
- and Aya is increasingly unwilling to imagine that leaving could be the responsible choice.

Ren's concern is simple:

> **The last time Aya loved a broken system this much, the system was my leg.**

He worries that McCluster has become another Project REN—something Aya believes she must personally save.

---

# 11. Ren and Jordan

Ren initially distrusts Jordan because Jordan's Wildcard behavior looks like exactly the kind of self-endangerment Aya feels compelled to manage.

Then he realizes Jordan is one of the few people who genuinely respects Aya's competence without needing to dominate it.

Ren also notices something Aya dislikes admitting:

Jordan understands the difference between accepting help and surrendering autonomy.

Ren and Jordan can bond around one principle from radically different histories:

> **You can save my life and still not own my next decision.**

Ren becomes one of the few people capable of telling Jordan when Aya is protecting him by trying to control him—and telling Aya when Jordan is using "freedom" as an excuse to make everybody else absorb his risk.

---

# 12. Behavioral priors

## When someone talks about him as inspirational
Default response: humor first, irritation second, direct boundary if it continues.

## When a safety system is described as compliant
Default response: ask what happens in the actual workflow under pressure.

## When Aya becomes controlling
Default response: resist early and explicitly; Ren does not let resentment quietly accumulate with her.

## When Aya is in danger
Default response: help if asked, but avoid reproducing Aya's own mistake of deciding for her.

## When authority invokes productivity against safety
Historical trigger: accident.
Response: severe skepticism; demand a concrete safety argument.

## When offered pity
Response: reject framing, not necessarily the person.

## When offered genuinely useful accommodation
Response: accepts it if it increases autonomy rather than performance for observers.

---

# 13. What Ren contributes to Aya's arc

Ren is living evidence that Aya's greatest act of love and one of her greatest acts of overreach can be the **same project**.

He prevents Aya's story from reducing to "control is bad."

Control helped build a remarkable prosthesis.

Precision mattered.

Persistence mattered.

Aya's refusal to accept "good enough" materially improved Ren's life.

The lesson is narrower and harder:

> **A good intervention can still become domination if the person receiving it loses ownership of the choice.**

That principle becomes central to Aya, Jordan and eventually post-Prime governance.
