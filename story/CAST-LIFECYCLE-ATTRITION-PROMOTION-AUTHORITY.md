# PRIM3 — Cast Lifecycle, Attrition & Promotion Authority

## Core principle

PRIM3 is a workplace/mission ensemble, not an anthology that freezes everyone except this week's owner.

The same people share buildings, labs, classrooms, command rooms, elevators, cafeterias, transport, debriefs, recovery spaces and mission support for years.

Therefore character development must happen **continuously and unevenly across the ensemble**.

Canonical law:

> **Every recurring character who appears in an episode must receive at least one meaningful development beat.**

A development beat can be small. It only has to reveal or change something that remains true afterward.

Examples:
- a joke reveals who somebody now trusts;
- a routine changes after an injury;
- somebody stops sitting next to a person they used to like;
- a worker quietly covers another person's shift;
- a character now asks for verification where they used to assume;
- somebody begins carrying a dead coworker's tool;
- a flirtation advances by one sentence;
- a resentment becomes visible in a hallway;
- an operator refuses a task they would have accepted last season;
- somebody who used to be support starts making decisions other people follow.

The episode owner still gets the deepest psychological write. Ownership is about **depth**, not monopoly.

---

# 1. Whole-roster continuity versus screen time

Do not force all approximately 39 immediate recurring slots onto camera every episode.

That would create roll-call writing instead of a believable institution.

Instead, every episode tracks the whole roster through one of four states:

```text
A — ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT
Appears materially and receives a state-changing/revealing beat.

B — AMBIENT CONTINUITY
Appears naturally in the workplace/world and receives a small specific continuity beat.

C — OFFSCREEN CONSEQUENCE
Does not appear, but something materially changes their schedule, workload, relationship, reputation, injury, assignment, debt or future availability.

D — UNAFFECTED / OUT OF FRAME
No meaningful write this episode; explicitly allowed, but should not persist for major characters across long stretches without reason.
```

The writer's ledger records one state for every recurring slot each episode.

This means “everybody is developing” does **not** mean every person receives equal dialogue every week.

It means the world never treats recurring characters like resettable furniture.

---

# 2. Minimum on-screen development density

Every ordinary episode should normally include:

- **1–2 episode owners** receiving deep development;
- **4–8 additional recurring roster characters** receiving smaller but persistent development beats;
- **0–2 established historical/outside/support characters** when a real relationship makes them useful;
- **0–2 opposing-board characters** when the mission naturally touches Hitman.

Typical meaningful named density across WATCH + PLAY:

> **7–12 characters.**

A larger episode can exceed this when mission scale justifies it.

The key test is not count.

> **If a named recurring character appears, what is newly true about them when the episode ends?**

If the answer is “nothing,” either give them a beat or remove the appearance.

---

# 3. Development beat classes

Every appearing recurring character should receive at least one of the following:

### BELIEF
Something strengthens, weakens or complicates what they think is true.

### RELATIONSHIP
Trust, resentment, affection, jealousy, debt, admiration, fear or obligation changes.

### COMPETENCE
They demonstrate, gain, lose or question a capability.

### STATUS
Their authority, reputation, clearance, assignment or informal influence changes.

### HABIT
A mundane behavior develops, disappears or gains new meaning.

### SECRET
They learn, hide, misinterpret or reveal information.

### MATERIAL LIFE
Money, housing, family, health, school, workload, transportation, schedule or career changes.

### MORAL LINE
They move toward, away from or across something they believed they would never do.

### GRIEF / LOSS
A prior death/injury begins affecting them differently.

### FUTURE SEED
A small choice creates the condition for a later major decision.

Micro-development is successful when it changes future writing, even if the audience barely notices it consciously on first watch.

---

# 4. Workplace recurrence law

The cast should overlap because they actually work together.

Recurring low-intensity scenes are mandatory texture:

- repeated elevator combinations;
- recurring cafeteria tables;
- lab rivalries;
- access-desk relationships;
- people borrowing equipment from the same person;
- somebody always being late to one specific briefing;
- informal mentoring across departments;
- post-mission recovery routines;
- people covering one another's shifts;
- shared rides;
- recurring jokes;
- maintenance complaints;
- public-relations people waiting for technical people to stop arguing;
- Pylon becoming part of daily rhythm before its behavior becomes alarming.

These scenes should accumulate.

A five-second beat in Episode 03 can become emotionally devastating in Episode 15 because the audience has watched the same two people do the same stupid ritual for years.

---

# 5. Support-character promotion law

Support status is **not permanent**.

A support character can become a main character organically when enough of the following accumulate:

- repeated appearances;
- distinctive competence;
- relationships with multiple mains;
- a personal problem independent of the episode owner;
- audience attachment;
- increasing mission responsibility;
- a secret or worldview that changes the larger story;
- somebody above them dies/leaves and responsibility moves downward;
- the person repeatedly becomes the one others call.

Promotion states:

```text
BACKGROUND RECURRING
    ↓
SUPPORT
    ↓
RELIABLE RECURRING
    ↓
SECONDARY MAIN
    ↓
EPISODE OWNER / MAIN
```

There is no announcement that a person “became a main character.”

The audience should realize it retrospectively:

> **When did this person become one of the people I care about most?**

---

# 6. Main-character demotion / drift law

The opposite can also happen.

A former main can:
- transfer;
- become injured;
- lose clearance;
- burn out;
- move to another site;
- become politically isolated;
- reduce mission exposure;
- become antagonistic;
- die;
- or simply stop being central while remaining alive in the world.

Screen prominence is dynamic.

Do not keep somebody artificially central merely because an early outline called them a main character.

---

# 7. Death / permanent-loss law

Missions are dangerous.

Named people may die.

The series should not protect every beloved character merely because the audience likes them.

But death must not become cheap shock arithmetic.

A strong death satisfies most of the following:

1. the character existed as a person before becoming a casualty;
2. the audience has seen ordinary life around them;
3. they have relationships with several recurring people;
4. their death grows from mission conditions, choices, institutional failure, sacrifice, bad luck or adversarial action already established by the story;
5. the death creates practical consequences, not only sadness;
6. different survivors interpret the death differently;
7. somebody's workload or authority changes because the person is gone;
8. rituals, jokes, empty desks, equipment, schedules and habits preserve the absence;
9. grief mutates across later episodes instead of disappearing after the funeral scene;
10. the story does not immediately replace the emotional function of the dead person.

Canonical line:

> **A death removes a person from the system. The system must then reveal what it had quietly depended on that person to do.**

---

# 8. No automatic replacement law

A death does **not** automatically produce a new equivalent character.

The first response to loss is often a vacancy.

Possible consequences:
- coworkers absorb work badly;
- missions deploy understaffed;
- Jordan gets called more often because a seam now exists;
- somebody receives a promotion they are not ready for;
- a specialist function disappears temporarily;
- a team becomes riskier;
- the institution delays hiring for budget/security reasons;
- people resent leadership for not replacing the person;
- people resent leadership when it finally does.

A replacement appears only when story/institutional logic makes it plausible.

The vacancy itself is story.

---

# 9. Replacement-entry law

When a new person eventually fills a role, they do not enter as “New Version of Dead Character.”

A replacement should have:
- different competence;
- different personality;
- different cultural/professional history;
- different relationships to authority;
- gaps the predecessor did not have;
- strengths the predecessor lacked;
- preexisting familiarity with **some** roster members but not all.

Canonical onboarding principle:

> **Nobody joins a workplace with equal relationship distance from everybody.**

A replacement may:
- know Helen from a prior project;
- have trained with Aya but never met Chioma;
- be somebody Miguel already distrusts;
- have been recommended by a support character;
- know one Hitman-side figure from a completely different life;
- arrive with a reputation nobody agrees on.

That uneven familiarity generates immediate story without pretending the newcomer is a stranger to the entire world.

---

# 10. Replacement social phases

A useful replacement arc follows:

```text
RUMOR
people discuss the incoming person before arrival

ARRIVAL
first impressions collide with prior reputation

COMPARISON
survivors unfairly compare them to the person who died/left

FRICTION
new methods expose hidden habits in the old team

USEFULNESS
somebody needs them before emotionally accepting them

BELONGING
private jokes / trust / debt begin forming

OWNERSHIP
they eventually receive a story that belongs to them rather than to the person they replaced
```

Do not skip directly from ARRIVAL to BELONGING.

---

# 11. Death does not equal replacement

Sometimes nobody replaces the dead person.

That may be better.

The organization can permanently change shape.

Examples:
- one function gets consolidated into another team;
- a mission type stops being attempted;
- automation takes over a task and creates new risk;
- surviving operators become more versatile;
- the vacancy becomes one reason Jordan's Wildcard role grows dangerously central;
- a support character gradually absorbs responsibility and accidentally becomes a main.

Permanent absence can be more emotionally truthful than clean staffing symmetry.

---

# 12. Support/history character restraint

Historical and outside characters remain important but are not quota filler.

Use them when they reveal something the workplace roster cannot:
- who the character was before McCluster;
- what family thinks of their career;
- what normal professional life looks like;
- what an old promise means;
- why a present reflex exists;
- what the institution has slowly changed in them.

Established examples include Hana, Ren, Tariq, Zayd and Bigga Lex.

A support/history character can graduate into the immediate recurring cast if they begin materially interacting with several current characters rather than only one person's backstory.

---

# 13. Attrition and gameplay

PLAY must support emotional investment without making every random tactical mistake destroy the hard story spine.

Use three casualty classes:

```text
STORY-LOCKED
A character's survival/death is fixed by current WATCH canon for a specific period/event.

CAMPAIGN-VARIABLE
PLAY can produce persistent injury, removal, transfer or death without breaking later hard-spine scenes.

PROTECTED-UNTIL
The character is not immortal; they remain non-lethal / severe-injury-only until a required story function has occurred, then can become death-eligible.
```

Protection should not be visible as arbitrary plot armor. The mission design should provide recoverable Downed states, extraction opportunities and Wildcard/Apex interventions.

Jordan and PRIM2 reduce cheap frustration. They do not erase permanent loss.

Royce and other high-pressure threats should make loss feel possible without converting every encounter into roulette.

---

# 14. Grief continuity law

When somebody dies or permanently leaves, update at minimum:

```text
WHO BLAMES THEMSELVES:
WHO BLAMES LEADERSHIP:
WHO BLAMES THE ENEMY:
WHO BECOMES MORE CAUTIOUS:
WHO BECOMES MORE RECKLESS:
WHO INHERITS THEIR WORK:
WHO INHERITS THEIR OBJECTS:
WHO INHERITS THEIR SECRETS:
WHO LOSES A ROUTINE:
WHO GETS PROMOTED:
WHO RESENTS THE PROMOTION:
WHO BECOMES LONELIER:
WHO ACTS LIKE NOTHING CHANGED:
```

The funeral, memorial or debrief is not the end of the death story.

The empty chair is a recurring character.

---

# 15. Episode cast-development ledger

Every episode must maintain two related ledgers.

## On-screen development ledger

For every named recurring character who appears:

```text
CHARACTER:
PRESENCE MODE: OWNER / ACTIVE / REMOTE / AMBIENT / OPPOSING / HISTORY
STARTING STATE:
DEVELOPMENT BEAT:
ENDING STATE:
CARRY-FORWARD:
```

No named recurring appearance graduates with a blank `DEVELOPMENT BEAT`.

## Whole-roster continuity ledger

For every current recurring slot:

```text
CHARACTER:
EPISODE STATE: A / B / C / D
CURRENT ASSIGNMENT:
HEALTH / AVAILABILITY:
RELATIONSHIP CHANGE:
WORKLOAD / STATUS CHANGE:
NEXT EXPECTED TOUCH:
```

The whole-roster ledger prevents writers from accidentally forgetting that a person exists for half a season.

---

# 16. Season-level balance

By the end of each season:
- every surviving Tier A character must have at least one deep write and several micro-writes;
- every surviving Tier B recurring character must have multiple meaningful development beats;
- support characters should have enough recurrence that at least one can plausibly rise in importance;
- every death/injury/transfer must alter staffing or relationships later;
- no major relationship may remain unchanged for an entire season without deliberate reason;
- no character may repeatedly appear only to deliver exposition.

The goal is cumulative attachment.

The audience should know who sits with whom, who annoys whom, who quietly covers whose work, who stopped laughing after a particular mission, and whose absence changes the sound of the building.

---

# 17. Governing lines

> **Episode ownership determines depth, not exclusivity.**

> **If you appear, you move.**

> **If you die, the building changes.**

> **If somebody replaces you, they inherit the vacancy—not your personality.**

> **The roster is not a list. It is a living social system.**
