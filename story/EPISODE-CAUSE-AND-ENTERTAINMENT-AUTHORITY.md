# PRIM3 Episode Cause & Entertainment Authority

## Core law

Every episode must answer three different questions with three different storytelling tools:

- **Human story: WHY does this mission matter and why must these people enter it now?**
- **Music film: WHAT doctrine, vocabulary, risks and expected plan should the team/player understand before entry?**
- **Mission: CAN the player apply that model when reality deviates from the plan?**

Canonical loop:

```text
INCITING HUMAN PROBLEM
-> PERSONAL / RELATIONAL STAKE
-> DECISION TO DEPLOY
-> MUSIC-FILM OPERATIONAL BRIEF
-> HUMAN ESCALATION / MISSION THRESHOLD
-> PLAYER BECOMES PRIM3
-> PLAN BREAKS / INFORMATION CHANGES
-> PLAYER ADAPTS THROUGH R/E/T
-> OPERATIONAL RESULT
-> HUMAN CONSEQUENCE
```

The series becomes weak whenever a mission begins merely because “an IT incident happened.” It becomes strong when a person, institution, relationship, prior choice, incentive, failure or hidden agenda creates the exact reason the technical operation must happen.

## Mandatory causality

Every episode must explicitly define:

1. Inciting event — what changed?
2. Human owner — whose life, trust, reputation, relationship or responsibility is affected?
3. Why now — why can this not wait?
4. Why PRIM3 — why this team?
5. Why these roles — why R/E/T all matter?
6. Expected plan — what the team believes will happen before deployment.
7. Hidden complication — what is unknown, stale, false, withheld or misunderstood.
8. Mission threshold — the exact decision/event after which the team is committed.
9. First plan failure — where reality attacks the briefing model.
10. Operational consequence — what changes on partial, poor or excellent execution.
11. Human residue — what relationship, belief, secret, guilt, authority or trust state persists.
12. Next-episode write — what future story now becomes possible or inevitable.

If these cannot be answered, the episode is not story-complete.

## Music-film briefing law

The music film is a stylized operational briefing, not a literal slide deck.

It communicates:
- the song's technical concepts;
- expected operating environment;
- intended doctrine;
- current assumptions;
- role responsibilities;
- major hazards;
- emotional thesis.

It shows **how the operation is supposed to work under the team's current model** without exposing exact enemy positions, hidden causes or exact lab solutions.

> **The briefing teaches the model. The mission attacks the model.**

## Mission-threshold law

Every episode requires a visually memorable point where story becomes operation: a channel dies, an alarm escalates, evidence starts disappearing, physical controls fail, a service crosses a critical threshold, a second actor appears, authority is granted/revoked or a person becomes trapped/in danger.

After the threshold, the player is inside the consequence.

## Technical problem must hurt something human

A mission objective may not stop at “fix the server.” Technical state must affect at least one meaningful human/story value:
- a person or population;
- a relationship;
- public safety;
- evidence/truth;
- reputation/trust;
- money/resources;
- an important service;
- command legitimacy;
- survival;
- a secret the characters care about.

The human story must likewise affect mission decisions rather than exist as detachable melodrama.

## Character-choice-before-crisis law

At least one meaningful complication per episode should normally trace to human/institutional choice rather than random failure.

Useful causes include:
- hidden mistakes;
- excess scope;
- deferred maintenance;
- cost optimized over resilience;
- convenience over verification;
- refusal to escalate;
- actions taken without consent;
- mission success prioritized over people;
- bad incentives;
- siloed responsibility;
- Prime deliberately withholding or shaping information.

## Relationship residue law

Every mission writes at least one durable relationship/story state. Possible writes include trust, authority, attraction, guilt, shared secrets, protection, imitation/mentorship, institutional belief, or increasing visibility of Prime's influence.

## Mission variety law

Do not let every technical episode become “reach server room and use terminal.” Across the campaign, rotate public events, campuses/corporate spaces, logistics chains, weather emergencies, operations centers, endpoint labs, remote sites, abandoned facilities, smart buildings, multi-provider environments, international/distributed events, recovery sites, live control environments, Site 0, dual-perspective reconstructions and post-assault continuity spaces.

Rotate pressure as well: armed opposition, environmental hazards, crowds/civilians, stealth/detection, service degradation, disappearing evidence, command conflict, time/capacity shortage, misinformation, unknown second actors, fatigue/injury and infrastructure cascades.

## Kinetic entertainment law

Combat remains first-class and satisfying, but a fight should answer what opportunity or state the gunfire is buying or destroying.

Examples:
- suppression buys T a clean validation turn;
- overwatch protects evidence or a fragile LAB_NODE;
- an enemy is stopped before destroying a rack or removing evidence;
- explosive entry is avoided because it would kill UPS/service state;
- restored lighting/door control creates a flank;
- the player must choose between pursuit and preserving service;
- an injured operator forces abandonment of an optional data objective.

## WATCH vs PLAY

- WATCH mission/action remains roughly three authored minutes inside the ~9-minute episode.
- PLAY expands the same canonical event into roughly 15–30 minutes for normal missions.
- Capstones may be longer/checkpointed.

## Story-complete schema

```text
INCITING EVENT
HUMAN OWNER
WHY NOW
WHY PRIM3
WHY R/E/T
EXPECTED PLAN
MUSIC-BRIEF DOCTRINE
HIDDEN COMPLICATION
MISSION THRESHOLD
FIRST PLAN FAILURE
TACTICAL/LAB CONSEQUENCE
OPERATIONAL END STATE
HUMAN RESIDUE
NEXT-EPISODE WRITE
```

**The story is not there to interrupt the game. The story creates the reason the game has to happen.**
