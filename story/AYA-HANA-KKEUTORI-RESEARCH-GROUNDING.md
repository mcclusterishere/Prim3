# Aya Shinoda / Hana Lee — Kkeutori Research Grounding

This file records the real cultural and linguistic grounding behind `story/AYA-HANA-KKEUTORI-BREADCRUMB-CANON.md`.

# 1. Japanese shiritori is real

`Shiritori` is a widely known Japanese word-chain game. Players say a word beginning with the final kana / sound of the preceding word. A word ending in `ん` is conventionally a losing move because ordinary Japanese words do not begin with that mora.

Sources:
- The Japan Times, Peter Backhaus, **Shiritori: a simple game that's great for practicing your Japanese vocab** (2017): https://www.japantimes.co.jp/life/2017/01/16/language/shiritori-simple-game-thats-great-practicing-japanese-vocab/
- Japan Foundation Erin's Challenge culture material identifying shiritori as the word game in which a new word begins with the previous word's last sound: https://www.erin.jpf.go.jp/materials/let-us-see/download/erin_lesson23_let-us-see_culture_quiz_en.pdf

Canon use:
- Aya plausibly grows up knowing shiritori.
- The `ん` dead-end rule is the real basis for Hana and Aya's later private N-rescue joke.

# 2. Korean kkeutmalitgi is real

Korean has an analogous word-chain game commonly called **끝말잇기 / kkeutmalitgi (also romanized kkeunmaritgi in some sources)** in which the next word begins with the ending Hangul syllable of the previous word.

Supporting source:
- General linguistic/game descriptions identify Korean `끝말잇기` as the Korean analogue of shiritori; contemporary Korean word-chain applications also implement the familiar no-repeat / continuation format.

Canon use:
- Hana plausibly grows up knowing kkeutmalitgi.
- Aya and Hana discovering that both cultures have a closely analogous childhood word game is plausible friendship texture.

# 3. Shared Sino-derived vocabulary is real

Japanese and Korean are distinct languages, but both contain large bodies of vocabulary historically derived from Chinese-character traditions.

Japanese uses substantial Sino-Japanese vocabulary associated with kanji. Korean contains substantial Sino-Korean vocabulary historically associated with hanja, even though everyday modern Korean is primarily written in Hangul.

Sources:
- Cambridge University Press, **The Handbook of East Asian Psycholinguistics** notes that Japanese and Korean have large bodies of culturally borrowed Sino-Japanese and Sino-Korean lexical items.
- National Institute of Korean Language, **Constructing the database of Japano-Korean Vocabulary** (2012), examining hundreds of Japanese-associated Sino-Korean lexical items and their transmission/history.
- National Institute of Korean Language, **A Contrastive Study of Sino-Korean, Chinese and Japanese Vocabulary** (2005), comparing same-form/same-meaning and related Chinese-character vocabulary across Korea, China and Japan.

Canon use:
- It is plausible for two highly educated multilingual engineers to enjoy comparing character roots, cognate-looking technical vocabulary, pronunciations and false friends.
- This does **not** mean modern Korean speakers routinely use hanja in ordinary texting or that Japanese and Korean vocabulary are interchangeable.
