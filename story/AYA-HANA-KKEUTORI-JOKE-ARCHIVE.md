# Aya Shinoda / Hana Lee — Kkeutori Joke Archive

## Purpose

These are actual recurring Aya/Hana jokes and dialogue seeds built around their private bilingual word-chain game **Kkeutori**.

The friendship, recurring concepts, N-rescue behavior and semantic clue payload are canon. Exact punctuation and micro-wording may be polished for performance/localization, but writers should preserve the joke mechanism and emotional reversal.

Real-language grounding is tracked separately in `AYA-HANA-KKEUTORI-RESEARCH-GROUNDING.md`.

Important rule:

> **The jokes must work as jokes before they work as clues.**

If a first-time viewer hears one and thinks "spy code," it is too obvious.

---

# 1. The first N-rescue — how Kkeutori is born

**Context:** Late lab night. Aya and Hana have already discovered that Japanese `shiritori` and Korean `끝말잇기 / kkeunmaritgi` are analogous word-chain games. They start playing ordinary shiritori in Japanese.

Aya, too tired to notice the trap, says:

**AYA:** パン. *Pan.* Bread.

Hana looks up immediately.

**HANA:** You lose.

**AYA:** I know.

**HANA:** 나무. *Namu.* Tree.

Aya stares at her.

**AYA:** You cannot answer `ん` with `나`.

**HANA:** I just did.

**AYA:** That is not how Japanese works.

**HANA:** Good thing I'm Korean.

**AYA:** That's cheating.

**HANA:** That's redundancy.

**AYA:** No. Redundancy follows the same specification.

**HANA:** Then call it failover.

Aya thinks about it, annoyed because she likes it.

**HANA:** Dead end in one language. Continue in the other.

**AYA:** I hate this rule.

**HANA:** So we're keeping it.

This becomes the canonical birth of the **N-rescue rule**.

# 2. The name Kkeutori

Several days later they are arguing over what to call their hybrid game.

**HANA:** 끝말잇기 is already a perfectly good name.

**AYA:** Nobody here can say it.

**HANA:** You can.

**AYA:** I said nobody useful.

Hana slowly closes her laptop.

**HANA:** Keep talking.

**AYA:** Shiritori plus kkeut. Kkeutori.

**HANA:** That is linguistically offensive to two countries at once.

**AYA:** Efficient.

**HANA:** Fine. Kkeutori.

**AYA:** You agreed too quickly.

**HANA:** I want written evidence that Japan caused this.

From then on they write the name inconsistently as `KKEUTORI`, `kkeutori`, `끝取り`, and sometimes simply `your turn`.

# 3. The MAP joke — 地図 / 지도

**Concept pair:** Japanese `地図` (*chizu*, map) and Korean `지도` (*jido*, map; historically 地圖).

During a design-review night Hana uses the shared character/concept bridge.

**AYA:** 地図. *Chizu.* Map.

**HANA:** 지도. *Jido.*

**AYA:** You cannot just say the same word in Korean.

**HANA:** I changed almost every sound.

**AYA:** The meaning is identical.

**HANA:** New interface. Same data.

**AYA:** That's not innovation.

**HANA:** Tell that to software companies.

Aya laughs despite herself.

Hana writes beside the chain:

`same map ≠ same view`

At the time it is a UI joke.

Much later, it becomes Aya's **MAP** breadcrumb: the map she was given of McCluster was real but incomplete.

---

# 4. The PERMISSION joke — 許可 / 허가

**Concept pair:** Japanese `許可` (*kyoka*) and Korean `허가` (*heoga*), the same Chinese-character compound meaning permission/authorization.

Aya has taken over a shared lab bench without technically reserving it.

**HANA:** 許可.

**AYA:** きょか. Permission. Your turn.

**HANA:** 허가.

**AYA:** Same characters again. Lazy.

**HANA:** Permission is multilingual.

**AYA:** Authority isn't.

Hana pauses half a beat.

**HANA:** That is a dangerous sentence from somebody who has illegally annexed three drawers.

**AYA:** I labeled them.

**HANA:** Ah. Then the occupation is legitimate.

The joke becomes recurring whenever Aya acts like she owns a workspace.

Years later the line **"Permission is multilingual. Authority isn't."** becomes disturbing: Hana was already thinking about the difference between visible permission and actual authority.

---

# 5. The ROOT joke — 根本 / 근본

**Concept pair:** Japanese `根本` (*konpon*, root/fundamental basis) and Korean `근본` (*geunbon*, root/foundation), sharing 根本.

Aya uses `根本 / konpon` during a character-bridge round. It ends in Japanese `ん`, which should kill the turn.

Hana immediately writes:

`근본`

**AYA:** Absolutely not.

**HANA:** Absolutely yes.

**AYA:** You rescued 根本 with 根本.

**HANA:** Same root. Different system.

**AYA:** That's cheating twice.

**HANA:** That's interoperability.

**AYA:** That's not what interoperability means.

**HANA:** It is if I win.

Hana circles the shared characters `根本` and draws a tiny root underneath them.

This remains a harmless running joke for years.

Later Aya recognizes this as the most literal breadcrumb in the sequence:

> **Same root. Different system.**

# 6. The PULSE joke — 脈拍 / 맥박

**Concept pair:** Japanese `脈拍` (*myakuhaku*) and Korean `맥박` (*maekbak*), the same character compound for pulse.

Aya is testing biosignal input and complains that the signal is noisy.

**HANA:** 脈拍.

**AYA:** 맥박.

Hana points at her.

**HANA:** Illegal. You are Japanese today.

**AYA:** I contain multitudes.

**HANA:** You contain one coffee and no lunch.

Aya checks the clock and realizes Hana is right.

**AYA:** Same characters. Completely different sound.

**HANA:** Like engineers and managers.

**AYA:** No. These still share the meaning.

Hana laughs hard enough to lose the next turn.

She later writes in the margin:

`if the signal is alive, watch the pulse`

At first it refers to Aya's sensor work.

Later it becomes the **PULSE** breadcrumb toward a living authenticated root identity.

---

# 7. The ZERO joke — 零

Aya has numbered prototype folders `0, 1, 2, 3`.

**HANA:** Why does everything you make begin at zero?

**AYA:** Because zero exists.

**HANA:** So does daylight. You don't use that either.

Aya ignores her.

Hana writes `零`.

**AYA:** れい. *Rei.*

**HANA:** 영. *Yeong.*

**AYA:** Same character. Different reading.

**HANA:** Zero is apparently the only thing engineers can inherit internationally without a meeting.

**AYA:** Zero is a valid first index.

**HANA:** That's exactly what someone who names a secret basement "Site 0" would say.

At the time **Site 0 does not mean anything to Aya**; Hana presents it as a deliberately absurd engineer joke about starting everything at zero.

Aya later remembers that Hana almost never made jokes about McCluster site numbering before this period.

This becomes the **ZERO** marker.

# 8. The INHERIT joke — 継承 / 계승

**Concept pair:** Japanese `継承` (*keishō*) and Korean `계승` (*gyeseung*), cognate character vocabulary for succession/inheritance.

They are fixing somebody else's terrible old control code.

**AYA:** 継承.

**HANA:** 계승.

**AYA:** Inheritance is too polite a word for this code.

**HANA:** What would you call it?

**AYA:** A hereditary disease.

**HANA:** Yet you're maintaining it.

**AYA:** Curses are billable.

Hana adds underneath:

`inheritance transfers the problem, not just the privilege`

Aya thinks Hana is talking about legacy software.

Hana is also thinking about the Mantle.

This becomes **INHERIT**.

---

# 9. The TWO joke — 二

**Concept pair:** Japanese `二 / ni` and Korean `이 / i` for the Sino-derived reading of two.

Hana holds up the character `二`.

**AYA:** に.

**HANA:** 이.

**AYA:** Two languages. One character.

**HANA:** Two engineers. One opinion.

**AYA:** We have two opinions.

**HANA:** Yes. Mine and the one you'll have after testing.

Aya throws a pen cap at her.

Later Hana adds a strangely specific line to an old note:

`two is a person if the number moves when the person does`

Aya originally assumes this is Hana being obnoxiously philosophical about operator IDs.

Much later it becomes the **TWO** breadcrumb: `2` follows a human holder rather than a software release.

# 10. The ramen incident — suspicious only in retrospect

Hana is good enough at shiritori that Aya becomes irritated whenever she deliberately chooses an obvious losing word.

**HANA:** ラーメン.

**AYA:** No.

**HANA:** What?

**AYA:** You didn't forget the rule.

**HANA:** Maybe I wanted ramen.

**AYA:** You just killed the chain for noodles.

**HANA:** Worth it.

**AYA:** You are an embarrassment to Korea.

**HANA:** Korea didn't invent ramen.

**AYA:** Then you're embarrassing multiple countries again.

Hana performs an N-rescue and changes the subject.

Years later, Aya notices this exchange sits immediately beside one of the marked character-bridge clues.

The joke still works. The loss was not accidental.

# 11. Tariq witnesses Kkeutori

Tariq walks into the lab while Aya and Hana are arguing over whether a cross-language bridge is legal.

**TARIQ:** Are you two playing a game or having a fight?

**HANA:** Yes.

**AYA:** She's losing.

**HANA:** Aya's language ran out of exits.

**AYA:** Your rules keep changing.

**HANA:** That's what exits are for.

**TARIQ:** I regret asking.

**AYA:** Good. Your turn.

**TARIQ:** I don't speak either language.

**HANA:** Perfect. Neutral judge.

**TARIQ:** That is not what neutral means.

Both women, simultaneously:

**AYA / HANA:** Exactly.

This is useful because Tariq remembers Kkeutori as something ridiculous and affectionate, not secretive.

# 12. Zayd tries once

Zayd joins a round while dating Aya.

Aya explains the rules for too long.

**ZAYD:** So I say a word, then she says a word, and eventually you tell both of us we violated a specification that did not exist five minutes ago.

**HANA:** He's learning.

**AYA:** There are rules.

**ZAYD:** You have a spreadsheet called `RULES_v7_FINAL_FINAL2`.

Aya freezes.

Hana nearly falls out of her chair laughing.

**HANA:** Marry him.

**AYA:** You're both disqualified.

Years later this memory is painful because Hana's laugh is one of the things Aya remembers most clearly.

# 13. The hidden pattern — Hana starts losing too often

After Hana realizes somebody above McCluster may be learning from her investigation, her behavior changes subtly.

She begins intentionally ending more Japanese-side turns in `ん`.

The individual incidents remain comic:

- パン / *pan* — bread;
- ラーメン / *ramen* — ramen;
- みかん / *mikan* — mandarin orange;
- きりん / *kirin* — giraffe;
- エンジン / *enjin* — engine;
- パソコン / *pasokon* — personal computer.

Aya teases her for becoming inexplicably bad at a game she taught Aya to take seriously.

**AYA:** You're getting worse.

**HANA:** Or you're getting better.

**AYA:** Those aren't mutually exclusive.

**HANA:** See? Better.

The first time through, it is friendship.

On reread, Aya realizes Hana is deliberately manufacturing **N-rescue markers** around the MAP / PERMISSION / ROOT / PULSE / ZERO / INHERIT / TWO clusters.

# 14. A joke that becomes terrifying — “wrong system”

Late in Hana's life, after another N-rescue:

**AYA:** You're abusing your own rule.

**HANA:** The rule exists because your system says the chain is dead when it isn't.

**AYA:** My language says it is dead.

**HANA:** Exactly.

Aya looks up.

**AYA:** That sounded dramatic.

**HANA:** I'm Korean. We have television too.

Aya laughs and moves on.

Years later she replays the line repeatedly:

> **The rule exists because your system says the chain is dead when it isn't.**

It was simultaneously a word-game joke and Hana's warning about trusting the system that defines what counts as possible, authorized, alive or finished.

# 15. The last unfinished chain

Shortly before Operation GLASSHOUSE, Hana leaves Aya one final Kkeutori turn in an otherwise ordinary message thread.

The surrounding conversation is mundane: food, a technical complaint, a joke about Aya sleeping at the lab again.

Hana's final game word ends in:

> **ん**

No rescue follows.

Aya sends a teasing response later:

**AYA:** You lost.

Hana never answers the game.

Hana dies.

For years, Aya remembers this simply as the last unfinished stupid thing between them.

When Aya later discovers the deliberate N-rescue pattern, the meaning changes.

Hana did not forget to continue.

She left the rescue open.

The emotional realization is:

> **Hana's last move was not "I lose." It was "your turn."**

Aya—not NIS, not Prime, not a cryptographic key—holds the final piece required to interpret what Hana meant.

# 16. Aya's reconstruction scene

When Aya finally sees the pattern, she does not instantly decode a spy message.

She notices a statistical/personality anomaly first:

> Hana was too good at Kkeutori to hit `ん` this often by accident.

Aya reorders the marked rescue clusters chronologically.

The semantic concepts recur:

```text
MAP
PERMISSION
ROOT
PULSE
ZERO
INHERIT
TWO
```

Then Aya remembers the old jokes attached to each one.

The jokes become instructions for **how to think**, not where to click:

```text
MAP       -> SAME MAP DOES NOT MEAN SAME VIEW
PERMISSION-> VISIBLE PERMISSION IS NOT ROOT AUTHORITY
ROOT      -> SAME ROOT CAN APPEAR THROUGH DIFFERENT SYSTEMS
PULSE     -> THE ROOT BEHAVES LIKE SOMETHING ALIVE
ZERO      -> THE ORIGIN IS INDEXED FROM ZERO / SITE 0 MATTERS
INHERIT   -> THE ROOT CAN PASS FROM HOLDER TO HOLDER
TWO       -> THE CURRENT NUMBER DESCRIBES A PERSON
```

Aya's first conclusion is not yet "Hana was NIS."

It is:

> **Hana knew. I was right the first time.**

That sentence destroys two years of Aya's institutional self-correction.

# 17. Writing rule

Kkeutori exchanges should recur sparingly throughout PRIM3 before the full reveal.

Use them as:
- affectionate flashbacks;
- Aya/Hana characterization;
- cultural/language texture;
- dry comedy;
- a visual motif in old messages/notebooks;
- and eventually a breadcrumb architecture.

Do not make every old joke secretly meaningful.

Most of their games must remain genuinely meaningless fun, or the friendship becomes retroactively fake and the hidden pattern becomes implausibly easy to detect.

Canonical ratio principle:

> **Hundreds of ordinary turns. A small number of deliberate dead ends. Seven meaningful clusters. One unfinished rescue.**
