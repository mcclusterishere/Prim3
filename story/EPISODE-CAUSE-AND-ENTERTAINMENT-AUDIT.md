# PRIM3 Episode Cause & Entertainment Audit

## Core recommendation

Every episode should answer three different questions with three different storytelling tools:

- **Human story: WHY does this mission matter and why must these people enter it now?**
- **Music film: WHAT doctrine, vocabulary, risks and expected plan should the team/player understand before entry?**
- **Mission: CAN the player apply that model when reality deviates from the plan?**

The clean episode loop is therefore:

```text
INCITING HUMAN PROBLEM
-> PERSONAL / RELATIONAL STAKE
-> DECISION TO DEPLOY
-> MUSIC-FILM OPERATIONAL BRIEF
-> MISSION ENTRY
-> PLAN BREAKS / INFORMATION CHANGES
-> PLAYER ADAPTS THROUGH R/E/T
-> OPERATIONAL RESULT
-> HUMAN CONSEQUENCE
```

The series becomes weaker whenever the mission begins because “an IT incident happened.”
It becomes stronger when a person, institution, relationship, prior choice or hidden agenda creates the exact reason the technical mission must happen.

## 1. Narrative causality rule

Every episode must explicitly define:

1. **Inciting event** — what changed?
2. **Human owner** — whose life/reputation/relationship/responsibility is affected?
3. **Why now** — why can this not wait?
4. **Why PRIM3** — why is this team the one being sent?
5. **Why these roles** — why do R/E/T all matter?
6. **Expected plan** — what does the team believe will happen before deployment?
7. **Hidden complication** — what is not known or is misunderstood?
8. **Mission threshold** — the exact choice/order/event after which the team is committed.
9. **Operational consequence** — what changes if they succeed partially or fail?
10. **Human residue** — what relationship/belief/secret changes after the mission?

If any of these are blank, the episode is not story-complete.

## 2. Music-film briefing law

The music film is a stylized operational briefing, not a literal slide deck.

It should communicate:
- the song's key technical concepts;
- the expected operating environment;
- the intended doctrine;
- the team's assumptions;
- role responsibilities;
- major hazards;
- the emotional thesis of the episode.

The music film should show **how the operation is supposed to work under the team's current model**.

It must not expose the mission's exact solution.

Canonical dramatic rule:

> **The briefing teaches the model. The mission attacks the model.**

Examples:
- `Got Wifi` teaches that signal, backhaul, authentication, interference and physical conditions all matter. The mission then creates overlapping failures so “strong signal” is not enough.
- `Data Breach` teaches indicators, containment, evidence and identity. The mission then forces containment while the people represented by the data are physically present.
- `RAID (Hot Site)` teaches redundancy/failover. Site 0 then weaponizes redundancy so some failover paths protect life while others preserve Prime's control.

## 3. Mission threshold

Every episode needs a visually memorable moment where story becomes operation.

Examples:
- an alarm changes from advisory to incident;
- a responder channel goes dark;
- the doors stop responding;
- evidence begins disappearing;
- the service demand graph spikes beyond capacity;
- a second unknown team appears;
- Prime's authorization mark is discovered;
- a human order commits the team to entry.

After the threshold, there is no casual return to the earlier conversation. The player is now inside the consequence.

## 4. Entertainment rule: the technical problem must hurt something human

The mission objective should never be “fix the server” without a human consequence.

Technical state should threaten or affect at least one of:
- a person;
- a relationship;
- public safety;
- evidence/truth;
- reputation/trust;
- money/resources;
- continuity of an important service;
- command legitimacy;
- survival;
- a secret the characters care about.

Likewise, the human story should not be detachable melodrama. Its conflict should alter mission decisions.

## 5. Entertainment rule: character choice before crisis

At least one meaningful mission complication per episode should trace back to a human decision, not random system failure.

Good sources:
- somebody hid a mistake;
- somebody exceeded scope;
- somebody optimized cost over resilience;
- somebody trusted the wrong source;
- somebody refused to escalate;
- somebody acted without consent;
- somebody delayed maintenance;
- somebody chose convenience over verification;
- somebody prioritized mission success over people;
- Prime deliberately withheld information.

This makes technical failure a consequence of character and institution rather than a lesson-generator.

## 6. Entertainment rule: the mission changes the relationship

Every mission should leave at least one durable relationship state change.

Possible writes:
- trust rises/falls;
- authority is accepted/challenged;
- attraction deepens/fractures;
- guilt attaches to a character;
- a secret becomes shared;
- somebody becomes protective of somebody else;
- a trainee imitates Jordan;
- Jordan begins/ends trusting an institution;
- Prime's unseen influence becomes more legible.

## 7. Current storyline strength audit

### Strongest current episode concepts

These already combine technical stakes, human stakes and continuity unusually well:

- **S2E3 — Dead Air:** technical victory paired with irreversible human loss.
- **S3E1 — Exposure:** breach response becomes personal because the cohort is inside the compromised data.
- **S4E3 — House of Eyes:** infrastructure directly controls doors, air and bodies; Prime arrives after apparent resolution.
- **S5E3 — Elastic:** technical scaling reveals another hidden operational actor.
- **S6E2 — Trust Boundary:** patch validation mirrors broken interpersonal trust without magically resolving it.
- **S7E1 — Hot Site:** every infrastructure concept becomes survival/authority under attack.
- **S7E2 — Mirror Room:** gameplay structure itself delivers the narrative reveal through opposite viewpoints.
- **S7E3 — Last Process:** the final technical problem is inseparable from the moral question of authority/personhood.

### Good foundations that need stronger causal triggers

- **S1E1 — The Blip:** strong discovery premise; should more explicitly tie the anomaly to a person's earlier choice or hidden Prime test.
- **S1E2 — Permission Slip:** excellent theme, but an onboarding staging incident may feel too low-stakes unless an authorized exercise collides with a real service/person consequence.
- **S1E3 — Open Book:** good investigative play; needs a stronger human reason the missing equipment matters now beyond assignment completion.
- **S2E1 — The Human Layer:** strong interpersonal conflict; the infiltrator's goal should materially threaten someone/something the audience already values.
- **S2E2 — Four Colors:** conceptually clever; should produce a memorable cost for staying in “exercise mode” too long.
- **S3E2 — Bad Input:** already strong because Jordan disobeys Chioma; make the player's technical shortcut visibly save people while simultaneously proving Chioma's fear valid.
- **S3E3 — Patient Zero:** excellent shame/blame mechanism; identify the person who hid the mistake earlier enough that the audience emotionally understands why.
- **S4E1 — Clean Install:** relationship metaphor is strong; mission trigger needs a more urgent human consequence than generic remote-site migration.
- **S4E2 — Dumpster Signal:** mystery is strong; give the unexplained facility evidence an immediate consequence or pursuer so it is not only archaeology.
- **S5E1 — Shared Responsibility:** technically useful but presently abstract; attach the failing service to a concrete operation/person and make vendor finger-pointing cost actual time.
- **S5E2 — Own the Exit:** strongest thematic risk of feeling like a business-school case. Make the CapEx/OpEx decision determine control of a physical extraction/recovery capability under pressure.
- **S6E1 — Road Kit:** excellent operator-life theme; give the remote recovery site's minimum viable environment a named human population/service that fails if Jordan overworks the team or chooses the wrong hardware priority.

### Open / capstone bridge

- **S6E3 / Song #21:** the working `Chain of Trust / Trust Anchor` concept is well-positioned to create the decision that Prime must be reached. The mission should not merely finish Security+/Network+ objectives; it should reveal that the institution's technical trust hierarchy and its human authority hierarchy share the same dangerous single root.

## 8. Mission variety rule

Do not let every technical episode end in “reach server room and use terminal.”

Across the 21 episodes rotate among:
- crowded public event;
- campus/corporate space;
- moving convoy/logistics chain;
- weather emergency;
- operations center;
- dispatch/service floor;
- mixed endpoint/lab environment;
- remote migration site;
- abandoned/decommissioned facility;
- smart building;
- multi-provider/global service environment;
- procurement/resource crisis;
- international distributed operation;
- field recovery site;
- live production/control environment;
- Site 0 multi-floor assault;
- reconstructed dual-perspective flashback;
- post-assault continuity core.

Mission pressure should likewise rotate among:
- armed opposition;
- environmental hazard;
- crowd/civilian pressure;
- stealth/detection;
- service degradation;
- disappearing evidence;
- command conflict;
- time/capacity shortage;
- misinformation;
- unknown second actor;
- fatigue/injury;
- infrastructure cascade.

## 9. Kinetic entertainment rule

Combat remains fun and visually satisfying, but every fight should be attached to mission state.

Examples:
- suppress a corridor to buy T one clean validation turn;
- eliminate a hostile before they destroy an evidence rack;
- avoid explosive entry because it would disable the UPS room;
- use technical control of lighting/doors to create a flank;
- decide whether to pursue an enemy or preserve a failing service;
- protect a moving technician while R keeps a route current;
- sacrifice an optional data objective to extract an injured operator.

A firefight is strongest when the player understands **what technical/human opportunity the bullets are buying or destroying**.

## 10. PLAY runtime vs episode runtime

The authored episode can retain a short mission/action segment for WATCH mode.

PLAY mode should not be forced into the same three-minute duration.

Recommended:
- WATCH mission montage: ~3 minutes inside the authored episode.
- PLAY mission: ~15-30 minutes for normal missions.
- capstones: longer/checkpointed.

The WATCH cut represents the canonical dramatic path. PLAY expands the same event into tactical decision space.

## 11. Definition of story-complete

Before an episode is considered fully authored it must contain:

```text
INCITING EVENT
HUMAN OWNER
WHY NOW
WHY PRIM3
WHY R/E/T
MISSION THRESHOLD
EXPECTED PLAN
MUSIC-BRIEF DOCTRINE
HIDDEN COMPLICATION
FIRST PLAN FAILURE
TACTICAL/LAB CONSEQUENCE
OPERATIONAL END STATE
HUMAN RESIDUE
NEXT-EPISODE WRITE
```

The story is not there to interrupt the game.
The story creates the reason the game has to happen.
