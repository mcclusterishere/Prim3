# Season One — Human Spine

This file does not replace the existing Season One canon. It supplies the interpersonal layer needed to turn the season outline into drama.

## The emotional promise

The protagonist does not join McCluster Corp because he wants danger. He joins because he wants ordinary things: stability, legitimacy, useful work, money, respect, and proof that going back to school at 33 was not a mistake.

The tragedy of Season One is that the institution gives him many of those things before revealing the price.

## Movement 1 — Nobody knows where to put him

On campus, he is old enough to be mistaken for an instructor and broke enough to be hiding whether he can afford lunch. Younger classmates alternate between respecting his experience and treating him like somebody who failed at adulthood.

He knows practical infrastructure. They know fresh academic language. Both sides can make the other feel stupid.

The first friendships should come from small exchanges rather than destiny: borrowing a charger, fixing a lab mistake, covering a meal, arguing over a group project, walking to class because neither wants to admit they missed the bus.

## Movement 2 — McCluster Corp feels like rescue

The internship is visually and materially impressive. The protagonist gets good clothes, competent equipment, access to serious systems, and adults who seem to recognize what he knows.

That matters because he has recently lived without stability. The corporation does not initially feel sinister. It feels generous.

This creates emotional leverage: when suspicious things appear later, leaving means giving up the first institution that has treated him as valuable.

## Movement 3 — The cohort becomes a family by accident

The cohort should not immediately call itself family. They become interdependent through repetition.

They have:
- a group chat that begins as logistics and becomes social,
- one recurring cheap food spot,
- arguments over who is carrying weak group members,
- private study pairings,
- inside jokes about instructors and corporate language,
- romantic tension that other people notice first,
- resentment over who receives better assignments,
- one person who always has the tool somebody forgot,
- one person who is chronically late but excellent under pressure,
- one person who appears emotionally stable until the first real incident.

## Movement 4 — Prime is felt before he is understood

Assignments occasionally arrive with strange specificity. Permissions appear before anyone requests them. A problem somebody reported privately is fixed before the visible manager hears about it.

The cohort develops competing theories about who is actually watching them.

Most theories are wrong.

The protagonist is less interested in conspiracy than in why the system keeps predicting what he will need.

## Movement 5 — The first operation changes social gravity

The cohort enters a problem believing technical competence is the test.

It is not.

The real test is whether they recognize that the instructions contain a dangerous assumption and whether they trust one another enough to challenge authority before the assumption becomes lethal.

People reveal themselves under pressure:
- the confident person freezes,
- the quiet person becomes decisive,
- the rival protects someone they dislike,
- somebody lies about being hurt,
- somebody obeys a bad order because they need approval,
- somebody breaks procedure and saves a life,
- somebody does not come home.

## Movement 6 — Grief becomes bureaucracy

The corporation has a process for the death before the cohort has language for it.

Access is revoked. Equipment is inventoried. A workstation is cleared. Somebody asks for a password reset while friends are still processing the last conversation they had with the dead person.

This is where the protagonist first realizes the institution expected casualties.

The important aftermath is not a speech. It is residue:
- an empty seat,
- food ordered for one person too many,
- an unread message,
- somebody inheriting the dead person's task,
- somebody blaming the protagonist,
- somebody blaming themselves,
- somebody becoming more loyal to the institution because admitting the death was meaningless would hurt worse.

## Movement 7 — He stays

The protagonist staying should not read as stupidity or blind ambition.

He stays because:
- he needs the money,
- he needs the degree,
- people he now cares about are still inside,
- he wants to understand who anticipated the loss,
- and some part of him cannot walk away from a system that has finally admitted he matters.

That decision is the first true step toward Prime.

## Season-ending relationship consequence

When he discovers that somebody at the highest level selected him before he applied, the revelation should damage at least one relationship.

A cohort member who believed they earned their place cleanly may now suspect the protagonist was favored from the beginning. Another may realize they were recruited because of him. A third may wonder whether their friendship was observed and scored.

The mystery is no longer merely “Who selected me?”

It becomes: “Which parts of my new life were actually mine?”
